01
Where we are

The Brief works — the wall of text inside it doesn't.

Cecilia (P2) called the consolidation of outside visits a clear win. Nelson called CA "so good." The Brief is doing the right job.

What's failing is the presentation inside each section. Multiple sentences of prose per section forces the clinician to read where they should be able to scan.

Existing decision #1 (Working Group, 2026-06-22): "Surface data without editorializing — like the most humble med student. Trust is easily lost when the system makes clinical interpretations. Earn it incrementally."
Existing decision #15 (Kevin, David, Molly, 2026-06-30): "One experience, three data profiles — same UX pattern, different data density."

This proposal works within both of those decisions. We're not asking to re-open either.

02
1 Since-last-visit framing

Make the time window unmissable.

Every count on the Brief today ("7 specialist visits", "9 medications") raises the same question from clinicians: since when?

Corey (P3) flagged this repeatedly. Once he understood everything was scoped to "since last visit," the Brief made sense. The problem was that scope was implicit.

What we're proposing:

  • Header explicitly says "Since [last visit date]" — persistent across sections
  • Optional strip surfaces what's new since last visit — new dx, new meds, values trending in wrong direction
  • Every count in each section is implicitly scoped to that window; no ambiguity
Prior art: Gong's AI Briefer, Cotera, Sybill all lead with time-window framing. Corey's ask matches an industry pattern.

3 Clinical conventions on values

Match how clinicians already read data.

Corey (P3): "Flag it high or low… it's the way that every other lab result on planet Earth comes into you."

Today the Brief says "abnormal" — a generic label that hides direction. Direction matters clinically. Potassium high vs. low means different things.

What we're proposing:

  • H / L flags on lab values — the standard clinical convention
  • Delta from prior when a prior value is available in-window
  • Med change type at the list level — New / Stop / Dose visible before you click in
Consistent with decision #1: we're surfacing data, not editorializing. H/L flags are annotations on values, not clinical interpretations.

Prior art · why this direction is defensible

Sales prep already solved this problem.

Every mature pre-meeting brief in the sales space uses templated structure, traceable evidence, and structural spine. Nobody uses AI narrative topline. The pattern we're proposing is what the reference class converged on.

Granola

Meeting notes

Pre-meeting Brief designed for back-to-back meetings. Structural spine, filled in beneath. Scannable in seconds.

→ Structural spine (Improvement 2)

Gong AI Briefer

Rev intelligence

Time-window pickers, section-level lists, every element linked to source call/email. No narrative summary.

→ Time framing + traceability

Sybill

Sales AI

Meeting-type-aware sections. Bullet-list format. Explicit "since last touch" framing throughout.

→ Sectional structure (Improvements 1 + 2)
The strongest quote from that scan: Granola — "You write the structure. AI fills in the supporting detail." That's the pattern we're proposing to bring to CA's Brief.
06
How we'll know it worked

Success criteria.

Concrete signals that would validate the improvements or trigger a rethink.

1
Clinicians stop asking "since when?" Time-window ambiguity was the top comprehension issue in P3 testing. If concept-test participants after the change don't ask that question in the first 60 seconds, Improvement 1 landed.
2
"Wall of text" language disappears from feedback. Both P1 (Venis) and P2 (Cecilia) used the phrase directly. If it's gone from post-change feedback, Improvement 2 landed.
3
Med changes and lab flags don't require a click to comprehend. Corey specifically flagged this — the change type was hidden until drill-in. If clinicians can answer "what changed?" from the Brief surface alone, Improvement 3 landed.
4
The "help me figure out what's going on" ask does not disappear. This is the signal that IA fixes weren't enough and the deeper synthesis question needs a separate answer — likely in Assessments. Not a failure of this proposal, but the trigger for the next conversation.

Appendix · edge cases

What happens when the
data isn't clean.

Real patients don't have complete records. Feeds fail. AI generation takes time. These states need to be as designed as the happy path — otherwise clinicians see broken empty sections and lose trust.

E0
Edge cases · data availability

No data. Partial data.

The Brief has to hold up when the underlying feed is thin or missing sections. Silence beats fake summarization; incompleteness beats invented completeness.

E3
Edge cases · principles

Design principles across all edge states.

  • Missing ≠ zero. "unavailable," "not on file," "none in last 12 mo" — say why the section is empty, not just show a 0.
  • Errors are section-scoped. One failed section doesn't fail the Brief. Every error carries a retry + a raw-data escape hatch.
  • Time is always visible. "Generated 2h ago" · "since last visit" · "new since generation" — the clinician always knows what era of data they're looking at.
  • Progressive rendering, not spinners. Sections light up as they complete. The clinician sees the shape of the Brief immediately, even while content fills in.
  • Fallback is structural, not narrative. When AI fails entirely, we still know how to count medications and lab panels. That deterministic skeleton is the safety net.
  • Consistent with existing decision #1 — "surface data, don't editorialize." Edge states show the truth of the system rather than pretending things are cleaner than they are.
Why these matter for the leadership conversation: Vitale, Roque, and Quiñones all cited trust incidents rooted in silent failure — the system looked confident when it shouldn't have been. Designing edge states well is a direct answer to that concern.
